§ 6.001

A marriage should not end by one person’s command.

We work to reform unilateral no-fault divorce — the rule that lets one spouse dissolve a marriage without cause and without the other’s consent — through legislation, the courts, and public argument.

The Problem

The defect isn’t divorce. It’s unilateral no-fault.

A marriage is entered as a binding civil contract and recognized as a public status. Unilateral no-fault lets that status be dissolved on one private person’s say-so — neither for a public reason nor by mutual agreement. It is neither genuine public policy nor ordinary contract law.

What the law does now

Dissolution on demand

One spouse can end the marriage for any reason, or none, over the other’s objection. The objecting spouse has no defense: a court cannot weigh whether the marriage is truly over, because the statute has stopped asking.

What we propose

Cause, or consent

Where both spouses agree, no-fault divorce remains available. Where one objects and no fault is alleged, the marriage is not dissolved on demand. Fault-based grounds and protections against abuse remain fully in place.

Our Aim

One goal, pursued in order of reach.

We are focused, not maximal. Each step is defensible on its own and sets up the next.

I

Restore mutual consent

Convert unilateral no-fault into mutual no-fault by adding and clarifying a mutual-consent requirement — the most pragmatic, high-impact reform available. It restores the one thing the current statute removed: the non-consenting spouse’s standing to be heard.

Already law in South Dakota and Mississippi — we’re asking states to adopt a model that works.
II

Align the incentives

A spouse who dissolves a marriage unilaterally, without cause, should not then be advantaged by it. Alimony and custody benefits should flow through fault-based or mutual grounds — not from a no-cause exit one party imposes on the other.

III

Build a pluralistic future

Our long-term aim is a personal-status model — a genuine menu of binding marriage codes couples opt into, on the pattern of jurisdictions where several codes coexist. A far more serious version of covenant marriage: default commitments, not an optional checkbox.

The Constitutional Case

A question of law, not of any one marriage.

May a legislature create a cause of action that dissolves a fundamental legal status without regard to fault, and without default or mutual assent? Our lead case argues it may not.

Morgan v. Morgan

Filings
Published by Mary’s Advocates

Marriage is formed as a binding contract. In Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), the Court reserved the very question now before the bench: a law that lets “one party annul a marriage without the consent of the other.” That is unilateral no-fault, described before it existed.

The challenge rests on independent, structural grounds:

Contract Clause · Dartmouth Due Process Separation of Powers Equal Protection First Amendment Religious Liberty Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
