What Marriage Is For

Five traditions, one civil institution.

Before you can argue what the law of marriage should do, you have to know what marriage is. These short entries sketch the telos, the purpose, of marriage in each tradition, how it is handled in the civil context, and what makes each distinct in family formation and public life. They are summaries, not full treatments. We will expand them in further writing.

Eastern Orthodox Christianity
Marriage as sacrament, ascetical path, image of the Trinity

Telos

Marriage is a mystery (sacrament) in which a man and a woman are joined by God into a single, lifelong life. Its ends are the mutual salvation of the spouses, the right ordering of the sexual faculty toward self-giving love, and the raising of children in the faith. The marriage is iconic: it points beyond itself to Christ and the Church, and to the perichoretic love within the Holy Trinity.

Civil treatment

Historically, the Byzantine state recognized the Church’s canonical jurisdiction over marriage. In the modern Orthodox world, civil divorce is tolerated as a concession to human weakness, and the Church may grant ecclesiastical divorce on narrow grounds (adultery, abandonment, apostasy) with penance imposed on a party deemed at fault. Second and third marriages are permitted but accompanied by a penitential rite rather than the full crowning service.

Distinctive

Orthodoxy does not treat marriage primarily as a contract between the spouses. It is a bestowed grace. The Church neither invents the bond nor dissolves it lightly; it witnesses and seals what God has made. The state’s role is to recognize and protect this reality, not to redefine it.

Roman Catholic Christianity
Marriage as sacrament, indissoluble covenant, domestic church

Telos

Marriage between two baptized persons is a sacrament instituted by Christ, ratified by free consent and consummated by the conjugal act. Its ends, articulated at the Council of Trent and reaffirmed by the modern magisterium, are twofold: the good of the spouses (mutual help, sanctification) and the generation and education of offspring. Validly contracted and consummated, the bond is indissoluble.

Civil treatment

Catholicism accepts the state’s civil jurisdiction over the external effects of marriage (property, inheritance, custody) but insists the state cannot dissolve a valid sacramental bond. The Church may grant a declaration of nullity finding that no valid marriage ever existed, but it does not grant divorce. Civil remarriage without a declaration of nullity bars the party from receiving Communion.

Distinctive

The Catholic position is unusually clean on jurisdiction: the civil law may regulate the effects of marriage, but the bond itself is beyond its reach. This has become the strongest ecclesiastical counterweight to no-fault divorce in the Western world.

Islam
Marriage as contract, with strong public and religious character

Telos

Islamic marriage (nikāh) is a civil contract between a man and a woman, witnessed and typically accompanied by a dower (mahr) owed by the husband to the wife. Its ends include the licit expression of sexuality, procreation, mutual tranquility (sakina), and the formation of a household governed by Qur’anic norms.

Civil treatment

Classical Islamic law provides multiple dissolution mechanisms: unilateral repudiation by the husband (talaq), judicial dissolution at the wife’s suit (khula or faskh), and mutual divorce (mubarat). The husband’s unilateral talaq, especially the triple formula, is the closest classical analogue to modern Western unilateral no-fault, and it has been repeatedly curtailed: the Indian Supreme Court ruled triple talaq unconstitutional in 2017, holding it “arbitrary” and that it “allowed one party to break down marriage whimsically and capriciously.” India’s parliament criminalized it in 2019.

Distinctive

In countries like Lebanon, personal-status matters including marriage and divorce are adjudicated by religious courts under the law of each confession. There is no civil marriage; the state recognizes the religious proceedings. This is one living model for the pluralistic personal-status framework we argue for in the long term.

Judaism
Marriage as covenant, with strict formalities for dissolution

Telos

Jewish marriage (kiddushin) is a covenant that sanctifies the union. Its ends are companionship, the fulfillment of the commandment to be fruitful and multiply (pru u’rvu), the sanctification of daily life, and the raising of children in Torah. The marriage is sealed by the ketubah, a document detailing the husband’s obligations to the wife.

Civil treatment

In Orthodox and Conservative Judaism, religious divorce requires the get, a bill of divorce delivered by the husband to the wife before a rabbinic court (beit din). Civil divorce alone does not dissolve the marriage in Jewish law; without a get, a woman is agunah, “chained,” and may not remarry. The required mutual process stands in sharp contrast to American unilateral no-fault.

Distinctive

Jewish law insists on the formal, witnessed, bilateral character of marital dissolution. The husband must deliver and the wife must receive the get; neither can discard the other by private decision. The sacred and the contractual are inseparable.

Modern Feminism and the Sexual Revolution
Marriage as a constraint on individual autonomy

Telos

In the dominant late-twentieth-century feminist account, marriage is not a sacred bond or a public institution; it is a contingent arrangement between autonomous individuals, serving (and only to the degree that it serves) their self-expression and personal development. The ends of marriage are not reproduction, sanctification, or the ordering of generations, but the mutual satisfaction of the parties. When satisfaction ends, so does the obligation.

Civil treatment

Beginning in the 1960s, feminist and progressive legal theorists treated the fault-based divorce regime as patriarchal, intrusive, and demeaning. The remedy proposed was divorce on demand: no fault, no witnesses, no court inquiry, no obstacle to exit. California’s Family Law Act of 1969 was the first full enactment of this vision. Within fifteen years every state had followed in some form. The feminist movement was not alone in supporting the change (the organized bar supported it even more actively), but it supplied the moral argument that made the change respectable.

Distinctive

This is the one tradition in which no-fault divorce is not a departure but the fulfillment of the view of marriage held by its adherents. Marriage is a project of the autonomous self. If the self no longer wishes to remain, no outside authority, public or religious, has standing to delay the exit. The sexual revolution supplied the view of the human person. No-fault divorce was the legal mechanism that made that view the ruling law of American family life.
