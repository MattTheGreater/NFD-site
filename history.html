The Long View

The jurisdictional history of marriage.

No-fault divorce is not new. The history of divorce law is not a linear progression from strictness toward freedom. It is cyclical: periods of stability followed by crises of family breakdown, followed by reform, followed by renewed loosening. What is new is which authority holds the keys. Over four centuries, jurisdiction over marriage moved from church to state to the individual will. Below is the timeline.

1st century BC – 5th century AD · Rome

Augustan reform, Byzantine looseness, Constantine’s edict

Emperor Augustus criminalized adultery and required husbands to divorce adulterous wives. By the time of Seneca the Younger, Roman practice had decayed into unilateral divorce on demand: women “numbered the years of their lives by the number of their husbands.” In 331 AD Constantine imposed penalties on unilateral divorces without substantial cause while leaving mutual divorce untouched. These reforms did not hold; through the Byzantine era, either spouse could end a marriage for any reason and for no reason at all.

6th century · Justinian

The Justinian code

Justinian I attempted the most restrictive reform of all, abolishing no-fault divorce entirely, including mutual no-fault. His decrees were repealed within a generation.

9th – 16th century · Christendom

Ecclesiastical jurisdiction and the Reformation

Under the Holy Roman Empire, divorce was nearly impossible to obtain. Marriage was understood as a sacrament under the jurisdiction of the Church. The Protestant Reformers responded with their own theology: Martin Luther taught that marriage was a civil matter and that the state, not the Church, should regulate it. This was the first great modern transfer of jurisdiction: from the Church to the civil magistrate. In 1534, Henry VIII’s Act of Supremacy made this transfer political and personal. In 1563, the Council of Trent formally identified marriage as one of the seven sacraments.

1629 – mid-1800s · Colonial and early America

Legislative divorces and the constitutional prohibition

Divorce was first allowed in Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1629. The first divorce in the United States occurred in 1638. Rather than through courts, early divorces were granted by legislative act, a bill of divorce passed through the legislature. In the early-to-mid 1800s, state constitutions were amended to move the jurisdiction over divorce out of the legislature and into the judicial branch. These are the constitutional prohibitions on special laws granting divorces listed on our state map. Their point was twofold: the legislature was overwhelmed, and legislative divorces were understood to be arbitrary political decisions rather than judicial inquiries. Divorce was moved to the courts to secure uniformity, predictability, and equal treatment under the law.

1869 onward · India

Personal-status pluralism

India developed overlapping family codes keyed to the major religious populations: the Christian Divorce Act (1869), the Christian Marriage Act (1872), the Muslim Marriage/Divorce Act (1937), the Secular Marriage/Divorce Act (1954), and the Hindu Marriage/Divorce Act (1955). The Indian system does not grant religious authorities direct jurisdiction, but incorporates religious provisions into the civil code and oversees them through magistrates. This is one existing model for a pluralistic civil regime; our long-term policy aim draws on it.

1918 · Russia

The Bolshevik Family Code

Pre-revolutionary Russia recognized the Russian Orthodox Church’s jurisdiction over marriage, which was dissolvable only in extraordinarily narrow circumstances. In 1918, the Communist government passed a Family Code that eliminated the validity of religious marriage, created a secular civil registry, and introduced unilateral no-fault divorce at the request of either spouse. The results, poverty, alcoholism, incarceration, abortion, mass abandonment of children, were catastrophic enough that family-law reforms were begun in 1926 and 1936 to curb them. The 1918 code is the first full modern instance of state-imposed unilateral no-fault, and its consequences are still visible in Russia’s divorce rate a century later.

1943 onward · Lebanon

Religious-court personal-status jurisdiction

Since Lebanese independence in 1943, no civil marriage has been available in Lebanon. Marriages are performed by religious authorities and administered under the laws of 18 recognized religious communities. Issues of custody and divorce are generally decided by religious courts under religious law. This is the fullest living example of pluralistic personal-status law.

1966 · New York

Mutual no-fault enters American law

New York enacted the first American no-fault divorce statute: a mutual, separation-based ground requiring both spouses to agree. This was not the no-fault divorce the public would come to know. It would be another half-century before New York converted its mutual regime into a unilateral one.

1969 · California

The Family Law Act

Governor Edmund G. Brown established the Governor’s Commission on the Family in 1966 to recommend reforms. Its final report, authored largely by Assemblyman James A. Hayes, proposed abolishing the concept of fault altogether. The California Family Law Act of 1969 replaced all previous grounds with a single standard, “irretrievable breakdown of the marriage,” alongside incurable insanity. Governor Ronald Reagan, himself the first divorced U.S. president, signed it into law on September 4, 1969. California became the first American state with pure unilateral no-fault divorce.

1970s – 1980s · United States

Fifty states, fifteen years

Within fifteen years, every American state had adopted some form of no-fault divorce. Most adopted it unilaterally, following California’s model. The law was not demanded by the American public. It was developed and promoted by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws, a body made up almost entirely of lawyers, many of them in private family-law practice. At Texas committee hearings on a 2015 bill to repeal insupportability, opposing counsel admitted acting as the “lobbying arm” of the Family Law Section of the State Bar.

2010 · New York

Mutual becomes unilateral

New York converted its 1966 mutual no-fault statute into a unilateral one by amending D.R.L. § 170 to add a seventh ground: one spouse’s sworn statement that the marriage has been irretrievably broken for six months. The press called the 2010 statute New York’s “first” no-fault law. What actually changed was the removal of the consent requirement. New York was the last state to make the transition.

2017 · India

Triple talaq declared unconstitutional

The Indian Supreme Court struck down the Muslim practice of “triple talaq,” a form of instant unilateral divorce available only to husbands. The Court held the practice “arbitrary” and said it “allowed one party to break down marriage whimsically and capriciously.” India’s parliament criminalized the practice in 2019. Western media celebrated the ruling as a victory for women’s rights while continuing to defend the structurally identical mechanism of unilateral no-fault at home.

2019 · Pankoe v. Pankoe

The first SCOTUS cert petition

With research and drafting support from our network, Pankoe v. Pankoe carried the constitutional objections to unilateral no-fault to the U.S. Supreme Court. The Court declined review; much of its analysis lives on in subsequent cases.

2020 · Dycus v. Dycus

Nebraska Supreme Court

The Nebraska Supreme Court rejected procedural due-process and special-legislation challenges to the state’s no-fault statute, dodging the structural and religion-clause questions by treating them as not preserved for appeal. In the political aftermath, the Nebraska GOP adopted our policy item into the state platform.

2022 · England and Wales

First reform in fifty years

On April 6, 2022, England and Wales removed the fault-based cause of action from divorce proceedings and made the process entirely digital. Prior to this, no-fault divorce was unavailable in the UK.

2024 – 2026 · United States

The counter-reform begins

The Texas GOP Platform elevates “unilateral” no-fault repeal from a plank to a legislative priority (Item 211). Aikins v. Aikins presses the facial challenge in Oklahoma through to decision. Morgan v. Morgan is filed in Dallas County on structural grounds. State senators begin interim hearings on no-fault. Our organization and allies continue the long work.
