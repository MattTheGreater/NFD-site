Constitutional Litigation

Testing unilateral no-fault in court.

For over a decade we have supported litigants pressing one question up through the courts: may a state dissolve a marriage without fault, default, or the other spouse’s assent? Each case sharpens the record for the next.

301st District Court · Dallas County, Texas

Morgan v. Morgan

Cause No. DF-25-09534 · filed 2025 · jury trial demanded
Active

Our most significant pending matter. Respondent Jeffrey Morgan, a member of our network, answered a unilateral no-fault petition with a facial constitutional challenge to Texas Family Code § 6.001 (“insupportability”), seeking a declaration that the statute is unconstitutional and void ab initio — and that a void statute confers no subject-matter jurisdiction on the court. Notice of the constitutional challenge was filed with the court and the Texas Attorney General under Tex. Gov’t Code § 402.010. The complete docket is published by our ally Mary’s Advocates.

Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, Div. IV · appeal from Pittsburg County

Aikins v. Aikins

Nos. 122,288 (cons. w/ 122,440) · affirmed Nov. 3, 2025 · rehearing denied Jan. 5, 2026
Affirmed below — fight continues

A full appellate test of the constitutional theory. James Alan Aikins, pro se, challenged Oklahoma’s no-fault statute, 43 O.S. § 101(7) (“incompatibility”), as facially unconstitutional and void — arguing it dissolves marital obligations “without default or mutual assent,” squarely invoking the Dartmouth exception, and that “incompatibility” is an ideological question the state has no power to adjudicate. The trial court denied his motion without reaching the constitutional merits; the Court of Civil Appeals affirmed in an unpublished opinion, leaning on Williams v. Williams (1975) and the New York Gleason line. The value of the case is the record it leaves: five clean questions presented, briefed to decision, showing exactly where courts deflect the argument — and what the next challenge must answer.

  1. Is the Contract Clause applicable to divorce legislation under the narrow exceptions in Dartmouth v. Woodward?
  2. Does § 101(7) violate the Contract Clause by dissolving marital obligations without default or mutual assent?
  3. Does § 101(7) unconstitutionally regulate one spouse’s belief over another’s, violating the Speech Clause?
  4. Did the trial court err by denying the motion without addressing its constitutional merits or jurisdiction?
  5. Does an unconstitutional and void § 101(7) deprive the trial court of subject-matter jurisdiction?
Docketed at the Supreme Court of the United States

Pankoe v. Pankoe

2019 · certiorari stage
Concluded

Among the earliest cases we supported with research and drafting, Pankoe carried the constitutional objections to unilateral no-fault divorce all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court’s docket in 2019. The Court did not take the case — it takes very few — but the petition proved the questions could be framed for the highest court, and much of its analysis lives on in the briefing filed in Morgan and Aikins.

Nebraska

Dycus v. Dycus

Constitutional challenge to Nebraska’s no-fault statute
Concluded

A Nebraska challenge we were involved with for some time before the case was taken in a different direction by other interests. Its lasting legacy is political rather than judicial: in its wake, the Nebraska GOP adopted our policy item into the state party platform — proof that a case can move the public argument even when a court does not move the law.

The Through-Line

Every case asks the question Marshall reserved.

In Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), Chief Justice Marshall set aside the case of a law allowing “one party to annul a marriage without the consent of the other.” Unilateral no-fault is that law. Our litigation exists to make a court finally answer him.
