Constitutional Litigation

Testing unilateral no-fault in court.

For over a decade we have supported litigants pressing one question up through the courts: may a state dissolve a marriage without fault, default, or the other spouse’s assent? Each case sharpens the record for the next. Most of the losses so far rest on the rights framing. Our Dallas County case, Morgan v. Morgan, presses the question as structural instead.

301st District Court · Dallas County, Texas

Morgan v. Morgan

Cause No. DF-25-09534 · filed 2025 · jury trial demanded
Pending

Our most significant pending matter. Respondent Jeffrey Morgan, a member of our network, answered a unilateral no-fault petition with a facial constitutional challenge to Texas Family Code § 6.001 (“insupportability”), seeking a declaration that the statute is unconstitutional and void ab initio, and that a void statute confers no subject-matter jurisdiction on the court. Notice of the constitutional challenge was filed with the court and the Texas Attorney General under Tex. Gov’t Code § 402.010. The full docket is published by our ally Mary’s Advocates.

The main argument

Section 6.001 lets the court dissolve the marriage for any reason and for no reason at all. It does not regulate the conduct of married spouses; it endorses the viewpoint of the party who wants out. A statute that supplies no cognizable standard of conduct is not a judicial cause of action. It is a legislative act of divorce executed by the court.

Every state constitution we have surveyed, Texas included, prohibits legislative divorces. The jurisdictional question is whether § 6.001 lets the legislature do by general statute precisely what the constitution forbids it to do by special act.

Why rights framing was never going to work

Every published appellate challenge framed as an individual-rights claim has lost. Courts answer that the defending spouse has no vested interest in the continuation of a marriage. The structural challenge is different: it does not depend on anyone’s private interest. It asks whether the legislature had the power to enact this at all.

Oklahoma Court of Civil Appeals, Div. IV · appeal from Pittsburg County

Aikins v. Aikins

Nos. 122,288 (cons. w/ 122,440) · affirmed Nov. 3, 2025 · rehearing denied Jan. 5, 2026
Affirmed below. Fight continues.

A full appellate test of the constitutional theory. James Alan Aikins, pro se, challenged Oklahoma’s no-fault statute, 43 O.S. § 101(7) (“incompatibility”), as facially unconstitutional and void. He argued the statute dissolves marital obligations “without default or mutual assent,” squarely invoking the Dartmouth exception, and that “incompatibility” is an ideological question the state has no power to adjudicate.

What the court said and how it was misconstrued

The trial court denied the motion to dismiss without reaching the constitutional merits, calling it “untimely” and “disingenuous.” The Court of Civil Appeals affirmed on the burden-of-demonstrating-error standard and leaned on Williams v. Williams (1975) and the New York Gleason line for the proposition that marital rights are “inchoate or contingent” and can be taken away by legislation before they vest.

The misreading: Aikins did not argue a vested marital right. He argued the statute itself is void because it supplies no legal standard of conduct. The court responded by reciting that the defending spouse has no vested right, which answers a different question. This is the classic rights/structure confusion.

The value of the record left behind

Five clean questions presented, briefed to decision, showing exactly where courts deflect the argument and what the next challenge must answer:

1. Is the Contract Clause applicable to divorce legislation under the narrow exceptions in Dartmouth v. Woodward?
2. Does § 101(7) violate the Contract Clause by dissolving marital obligations without default or mutual assent?
3. Does § 101(7) unconstitutionally regulate one spouse’s belief over another’s, violating the Speech Clause?
4. Did the trial court err by denying the motion without addressing its constitutional merits or jurisdiction?
5. Does an unconstitutional and void § 101(7) deprive the trial court of subject-matter jurisdiction?

Nebraska Supreme Court

Dycus v. Dycus

307 Neb. 426, 949 N.W.2d 357 (2020)
Adverse precedent. Dodged the structural question.

A Nebraska challenge we were involved with for some time before the case was taken in a different direction by other interests. Michael Dycus, a Catholic who opposed the divorce, argued Neb. Rev. Stat. § 42-361 denied defendants procedural due process and operated as special legislation granting divorces. The Nebraska Supreme Court affirmed against him.

What the court held, and the fallacy

The court held that defendants receive their “day in court” and that the no-fault statute “does not deprive defendants of a substantial vested property interest, as marriage is not an ordinary civil contract but a personal relationship subject to dissolution on terms fixed by state law.” This is a tautology: it assumes the terms the legislature fixed are terms it had the power to fix, which is the very question posed. The holding doesn’t rebut the structural claim; it restates the legislature’s self-asserted authority as if that settled the matter.

The court declined to reach separation-of-powers, Establishment Clause, and Free Exercise theories because they were not specifically preserved on appeal. The constitutional case against unilateral no-fault was not fully presented. It still has not been.

The lasting legacy

Nebraska Republicans adopted our policy item into the state platform in the wake of the case. The litigation moved the political argument even though the court did not move the law.

Docketed at the Supreme Court of the United States

Pankoe v. Pankoe

222 A.3d 443 (Pa. Super. 2019); cert. denied, No. 20-590, 141 S. Ct. 1063 (2021)
Concluded

Among the earliest cases we supported with research and drafting. Pankoe carried the constitutional objections to unilateral no-fault divorce to the Pennsylvania Superior Court and from there to the U.S. Supreme Court’s docket. The cert petition argued 23 Pa.C.S. § 3301(d) is a “viewpoint-based statute” violating the Establishment Clause. The Court declined review.

What it accomplished

Even in loss, the petition proved the questions could be framed for the highest court. Much of its analysis lives on in the briefing filed in Morgan and Aikins.

The Through-Line

Every case asks the question Marshall reserved.

In Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819), Chief Justice Marshall set aside the case of a law allowing “one party to annul a marriage without the consent of the other.” Unilateral no-fault is that law. Our litigation exists to make a court finally answer him, and to answer him on the right question: not whose rights are being impaired, but whose power is being exceeded.
